# What If School No Longer Teaches Us How to Live — But How to Sell Our Time?
There is an idea that I haven't been able to stop thinking about lately.
Maybe modern school is an outdated tool.
Not because teachers are bad.
Not because children don't need knowledge.
And not even because the idea of school itself is wrong.
The problem may be something else.
**We are still using a system designed for one type of society to prepare children for life in a completely different one.**
There was a time when society needed large numbers of disciplined people capable of performing standardized work. The industrial era required punctuality, repetition, discipline, and the ability to work according to a fixed schedule.
And school was very good at teaching exactly that.
A timetable.
A bell.
A lesson.
An assignment.
The correct answer.
A grade.
The next lesson.
Again.
And again.
Little by little, a child becomes accustomed to a very specific model of behavior:
**someone decides what needs to be done — you do it — and if you do it correctly, you are rewarded.**
Then school ends.
And suddenly, you realize that this wasn't just an educational game.
It was a rehearsal for adult life.
You look for a job.
You go through an interview.
You get a position.
Someone determines your working hours.
Someone determines your tasks.
Someone determines your deadlines.
You sell your time to an employer.
Eight hours a day, give or take.
And at the end of the month, you get paid.
And this raises a simple question for me:
**Why do we teach children so little about another possible path?**
Not just:
> "How do you find a good job?"
But:
**"How do you create a job?"**
Not just:
> "How do you complete a task correctly?"
But:
**"How do you figure out which problem is actually worth solving?"**
Not just:
> "How do you get a high grade?"
But:
**"How do you create something valuable enough that people will want to pay for it?"**
Not just:
> "How do you become the best employee?"
But:
**"How do you build a system that other people can work in?"**

---

## We Reward Effort Too Much
In school, effort is one of the main virtues.
A child sits down.
Does their homework.
Doesn't arrive late.
Studies.
Memorizes.
Repeats.
Follows the instructions.
And gets a good grade.
But the adult world works a little differently.
If you spend ten hours diligently doing something nobody needs, your effort doesn't magically make the result valuable.
If someone else spends one hour finding a way to solve the same problem ten times more efficiently, their solution may be worth far more.
**Results are not always proportional to the number of hours you spend working.**
Yet for years, we teach children almost the opposite:
> "The harder you work, the greater the reward."
That logic works well in a system where the goal is to measure the completion of standardized tasks.
But what happens when the most valuable thing is no longer execution?
What happens when the most valuable thing is **creation**?

---

## And Then AI Entered the System
This is where things get really interesting.
AI can write.
It can calculate.
It can analyze.
It can generate images.
It can program.
It can perform an enormous number of repetitive intellectual tasks.
In other words, many of the qualities we have traditionally valued in a good employee:
**diligence, speed, repetition, discipline, and the ability to follow instructions**
— are becoming cheaper.
A machine can perform a task without getting tired.
Without taking weekends off.
Without asking for overtime pay.
And without getting offended by the boss.
So here is the uncomfortable question:
**If AI is becoming an excellent executor, why are we still building education primarily around producing good executors?**
Maybe this is exactly the moment when we should start teaching something different.
Not just how to answer.
But how to **ask questions**.
Not just how to execute.
But how to **invent**.
Not just how to find the right answer.
But how to **identify the problem worth solving**.
Not just how to work inside a system.
But how to **understand how systems are created**.

---

## I'm Not Saying Everyone Should Become an Entrepreneur
That would be just as absurd.
The world needs doctors, engineers, teachers, scientists, programmers, drivers, designers, builders, and countless other professionals.
And having a traditional job is not a bad thing.
The problem begins when **employment becomes the only model of a successful adult life that we show children.**
As if there were only one route:
**school → university → résumé → job application → employment → salary → retirement.**
But what about:
**school → idea → experiment → failure → product → own system?**
Why do we talk so little to children about the possibility of becoming more than employees?
They can become **creators**.

---

## Maybe We Need a Different Kind of School
A school where children aren't simply given assignments, but given problems.
Instead of saying:
> "Do it this way."
We ask:
> **"What can you come up with?"**
A school where we don't evaluate only whether the answer is correct.
But also the quality of the question.
Where failure isn't treated as defeat, but as information.
Where a child can create their own project, launch it, discover that nobody actually needs it, figure out why, and try again.
Where financial literacy isn't an optional extra.
Where programming isn't just about writing code.
Where AI isn't a magic button that says:
> "Do it for me."
But a tool that allows a person to **create more than they could create alone.**

---

## Because the World Is Changing Faster Than School
And perhaps the biggest problem with modern education isn't that children don't know enough.
Maybe it's that we are still teaching them **how to live in a world that is gradually disappearing.**
The world where people sell eight hours of their day to an employer isn't going away tomorrow.
But it is no longer the only possible model.
And if AI continues to take over routine work, the question won't only be:
**"Where can I find a job?"**
The much more interesting question will be:
**"What can I create that didn't exist before me?"**
That is the question I would like to hear from schools much more often.
Because perhaps the real purpose of education in the future isn't to turn a child into a good employee.
Maybe it is to give them enough knowledge, courage, and tools to eventually say:
**"I don't have to find a place in your system. I can create my own."**
